## Danazol: From Endometriosis to Amenorrhea (Menstrual Suppression)
{: .text-alpha }

## One-Sentence Summary

Danazol is an oral synthetic steroid originally used to treat endometriosis and fibrocystic breast disease.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **amenorrhea (menstrual suppression)**,
with **no registered clinical trials** but **20 publications** (mostly reviews and observational reports) currently supporting this direction.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Currently, detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Based on the published literature, danazol suppresses the pituitary-ovarian axis. It reduces gonadotropin (GnRH/LH/FSH) signalling and ovarian steroid production, and it produces an atrophic endometrium. These pharmacological effects underlie its use in endometriosis.

Amenorrhea is a direct consequence of this same action. Endometriosis lesions regress when the ovaries are down-regulated, as in amenorrhea or menopause, so inducing amenorrhea is part of how danazol treats the disease. The new indication is therefore closely tied to the original one and is a plausible extension rather than a distant mechanistic leap.

A 2024 retrospective multi-site cohort study supports danazol for menstrual suppression in transgender and nonbinary individuals. The evidence remains observational and indirect. Androgenic adverse effects, liver toxicity, thromboembolic risk and teratogenicity limit its use compared with newer options such as progestins and GnRH antagonists.

## Safety Considerations

- **Disease-level contraindications (from the knowledge graph, not label text)**: epilepsy, porphyria (including inherited porphyria and other porphyrin metabolism disorders), kidney disease, liver disease, gallbladder disease, female breast carcinoma, migraine disorder, and familial combined hyperlipidemia. Pyoureter is also listed.
- **Drug Interactions**: 100 interactions were found. The sample reviewed includes one major interaction, with **cisapride**. Most others are moderate, including antidiabetic agents (acarbose, SGLT2 inhibitors such as canagliflozin and dapagliflozin, GLP-1 agonists such as exenatide and dulaglutide, sulfonylureas such as glimepiride and glipizide, and insulins), budesonide, and eliglustat.
- **Other risks from the literature**: androgenic effects (hirsutism, voice changes, acne, weight gain), hepatotoxicity, thromboembolic risk and teratogenicity. A published case report describes rhabdomyolysis and pancreatitis when danazol was given with lovastatin.

Please also refer to the package insert for the official warnings and contraindications.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold (research question)**

**Rationale:**
The mechanism for inducing amenorrhea is well established and a 2024 cohort supports menstrual suppression. However, there are no registered trials, no RCT, and the evidence is observational or indirect (L3). Safety concerns and newer alternatives also weigh against prioritising danazol for this use.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Singapore package insert warnings and contraindications from HSA, which are required for safety screening
- Detailed mechanism of action data from DrugBank
- Prospective or comparative studies of danazol against current agents (progestins, GnRH antagonists) for menstrual suppression
- A risk-benefit assessment for hepatic, thromboembolic, androgenic and teratogenic risks, with pregnancy exclusion measures
