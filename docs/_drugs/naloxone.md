# Naloxone: From Opioid Dependence to Continuous Spikes and Waves During Sleep

## One-Sentence Summary

Naloxone is an opioid antagonist, originally used for opioid dependence and substance abuse, and it is the standard agent for reversing opioid effects.
The TxGNN model's top-ranked prediction is **continuous spikes and waves during sleep**, but **no clinical trials and no publications** support this prediction.
The best-supported alternative in the data compiled for this report is **hypoglycemia** (3 clinical trials, all terminated, and a 2025 clinical study of intranasal naloxone).

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data is not available. Based on known information, naloxone is a competitive opioid-receptor antagonist (mainly at the mu receptor). It is used to reverse opioid effects and to treat opioid dependence and overdose.

For the top-ranked prediction, continuous spikes and waves during sleep (an epileptic encephalopathy with electrical status epilepticus in sleep), no plausible link to opioid-receptor antagonism was identified in the data. The prediction rests on the model score alone. Notably, the knowledge graph also lists epilepsy as a contraindication for naloxone, which argues against this use.

Other predictions are more plausible mechanistically:

- **Hypoglycemia (rank 7):** Opioid signalling is thought to blunt counterregulatory and autonomic responses in hypoglycemia-associated autonomic failure (HAAF). Blocking opioid receptors may restore these responses. This is the best-supported direction, backed by trials and a 2025 clinical study.
- **Drug-induced dyskinesia (rank 9):** The opioid system modulates basal ganglia dopamine signalling. Naloxone reduced levodopa-induced dyskinesia in primate models, but human data are small and old.
- **Schizophreniform and psychotic disorders (ranks 4 and 6):** This direction comes from the 1980s endogenous-opioid hypothesis of psychosis, and results were inconsistent or negative.

The remaining predictions (lissencephaly with cerebellar hypoplasia, DECR deficiency encephalopathy, acute laryngopharyngitis) have no evidence and no apparent mechanistic link.

## Safety Considerations

Please refer to the package insert for safety information. The package insert warnings were not available for this report.

- **Contraindications flagged in the knowledge graph** (these come from drug-disease relationships, not label text): epilepsy, long QT syndrome, torsades de pointes, heart conduction disease, sudden cardiac arrest, orthostatic hypotension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, respiratory failure, kidney disease, liver disease, adrenal insufficiency/Addison disease, hypothyroidism, inflammatory bowel disease, hepatitis B/C infection and benign prostatic hyperplasia. The epilepsy flag is particularly relevant to the top-ranked prediction.
- **Drug interactions:** 88 interactions were recorded. Among those listed, droperidol is rated **Major**. Most others are Moderate, including opioids (fentanyl, morphine, codeine, tramadol, methadone, oxycodone, hydrocodone), peripherally acting opioid antagonists (methylnaltrexone, naldemedine, naloxegol) and binimetinib. Hydrocortisone is Minor.
- **Other:** A 1996 rat study reported naloxone-induced seizures in the setting of viral encephalitis.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The top-ranked prediction (continuous spikes and waves during sleep) has only a model score behind it (L5), with no trials, no literature and no plausible mechanism. Epilepsy is also listed as a contraindication in the knowledge graph. Among the other predictions, hypoglycemia (HAAF) is the most promising and could be pursued as a separate research question. However, its trials were terminated with very small enrollment, so efficacy is unconfirmed.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Re-evaluate the candidate list and consider prioritising hypoglycemia/HAAF (and possibly drug-induced dyskinesia) over the top-ranked prediction
- Full results and conclusions of the 2025 intranasal naloxone HAAF studies
- Mechanism of action data
- Package insert warnings and contraindications from the HSA, to enable safety screening
- Assessment of the epilepsy contraindication and the major droperidol interaction
- Route and formulation compatibility (for example, intranasal use for HAAF versus the currently registered injection and oral combination products)
