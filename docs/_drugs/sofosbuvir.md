# Sofosbuvir: From Hepatitis C Virus Infection to Hepatitis B Virus Infection

## One-Sentence Summary

Sofosbuvir is an oral nucleotide polymerase inhibitor, originally used to treat chronic hepatitis C virus infection.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **hepatitis B virus infection**, but the **11 clinical trials** and **20 publications** retrieved for this prediction are mostly about hepatitis C, and the evidence for hepatitis B efficacy is indirect and weak.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data is not available for this report. From known information, sofosbuvir is a nucleotide analogue that inhibits the HCV NS5B RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. Its efficacy in hepatitis C is well established, and it is marketed in Singapore alone and in fixed-dose combinations (with velpatasvir, ledipasvir, or velpatasvir plus voxilaprevir).

The mechanistic link to hepatitis B is weak. HBV replicates through a reverse-transcriptase (DNA polymerase) mechanism, so direct anti-HBV activity is mechanistically unclear. The high TxGNN score most likely reflects the shared class of hepatotropic viruses and the drug's HCV indication, rather than a true antiviral rationale.

Most clinical data come from patients co-infected with HCV and HBV. In these patients, HBV-DNA and HBsAg levels change during therapy as a secondary effect of clearing HCV. HBV reactivation during direct-acting antiviral treatment is also a recognised safety concern. One small phase 2 study of ledipasvir/sofosbuvir in HBV-infected patients (PMID 36045503) reported on HBsAg and HBV DNA changes. However, it was single-arm and small, and it used a combination product.

**Caveat on the evidence level:** The L2 label is based on the presence of a phase 2/3 study. That study (NCT02555943) is a small co-infection study of reactivation monitoring, not a trial of anti-HBV efficacy. In practice, the evidence for this specific indication is weaker than L2 suggests.

## Safety Considerations

- **Drug Interactions** (8 interactions recorded; major ones are the most important):
  - **Major:** apalutamide, enzalutamide, lorlatinib
  - **Moderate:** fostamatinib, warfarin, dicoumarol, encorafenib
  - **Minor:** cyclosporine
- **HBV reactivation:** The literature repeatedly reports HBV reactivation in HBV/HCV co-infected patients receiving sofosbuvir-based regimens. This is directly relevant to any use in HBV, and monitoring and prophylaxis need to be considered.

For warnings and contraindications, please refer to the package insert.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The prediction score is very high, but it is not backed by a plausible direct mechanism, since HBV uses a reverse transcriptase rather than the polymerase sofosbuvir targets. The available clinical data are mostly HCV studies, or HCV/HBV co-infection studies where HBV changes are secondary and reactivation is a safety risk. The one HBV-specific study is small, single-arm, and used a combination product.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Mechanistic or in vitro data on direct anti-HBV activity of sofosbuvir
- Controlled studies in HBV mono-infected patients that separate the effect of sofosbuvir from that of ledipasvir or other agents
- Mechanism of action data and package insert warnings and contraindications from the HSA
- An HBV reactivation monitoring and prophylaxis plan

**Other predicted indications (for reference):** Hepatitis E virus infection (rank 2) has the most direct evidence. A completed phase 2 pilot (NCT03282474, n=10) and in vitro work support activity, but the pilot showed only a transient viral load decline without sustained clearance. This may be a more promising research direction than hepatitis B. The remaining predictions (hepatitis A, Kyasanur forest disease, Omsk hemorrhagic fever, HIV, and animal or other retroviral conditions) have only in vitro evidence or no evidence, and the retroviral ones have no plausible mechanism.
