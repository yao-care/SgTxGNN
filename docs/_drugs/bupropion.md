# Bupropion: From Depression and Smoking Cessation to Attention Deficit-Hyperactivity Disorder

## One-Sentence Summary

Bupropion was originally used for depressive disorders (such as major depressive disorder), anxiety-related conditions and nicotine dependence.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)**,
with **6 clinical trials** (including 1 completed Phase 3 randomised controlled trial) and **20 publications** currently supporting this direction.

*Note on evidence level:* The data compiled for this report assigns L1. By the stated rules, L1 requires at least two completed Phase 3 RCTs. Only one clearly qualifying trial (NCT00048360) is identified, plus one Phase 2/3 trial (NCT00061087) whose intervention arm is not confirmed as bupropion. A conservative reading is therefore L2. Supporting evidence also comes from a Cochrane review of bupropion in adult ADHD.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. From the published literature, bupropion inhibits the reuptake of norepinephrine and dopamine and has no serotonergic activity. It is an antidepressant that also supports smoking cessation.

ADHD is linked to dysfunction of the noradrenergic and dopaminergic systems. Established ADHD treatments, such as stimulants (methylphenidate, amphetamines) and atomoxetine, act on these same pathways. Bupropion's catecholaminergic action therefore gives a plausible mechanism for improving attention and impulsivity. Reviews already list it among the non-stimulant options for adults who do not respond to, or cannot tolerate, stimulants.

The original indications (depression, nicotine dependence) and ADHD often occur together, and several trials test bupropion in people with ADHD and substance use disorders. This fits the very high TxGNN score.

## Safety Considerations

- **Contraindications flagged in the knowledge graph** (not taken from the package insert): hypertension, epilepsy, liver failure or liver disease (including cirrhosis), kidney disease, manic bipolar disorder, psychotic disorders (schizophreniform, schizoaffective), anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, opiate dependence, substance abuse/dependence, myocardial infarction, central nervous system neoplasm, and others.
- **Drug Interactions**: 100 interactions were found. Major-level ones among those retrieved include fentanyl, lidocaine, codeine, dihydrocodeine, tramadol, methylphenidate, hydrocortisone, aldesleukin, alfentanil and amantadine. Moderate-level ones include ethanol, alprazolam, nifedipine, hydrochlorothiazide, cetirizine and chlorpheniramine.
- **Guardrails for ADHD use**: dose-dependent seizure risk, blood pressure monitoring, and caution in patients with comorbid substance use or eating disorders.

Package insert warnings and contraindications were not available and should be checked.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Proceed with Guardrails**

**Rationale:**
A completed Phase 3 placebo-controlled trial, several further ADHD trials and a Cochrane review support the use of bupropion in adult ADHD, and its mechanism is plausible. Seizure risk, blood pressure effects and the many knowledge-graph contraindications call for a controlled approach.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Singapore package insert warnings and contraindications (a blocking gap for safety screening)
- Confirmation of bupropion as the intervention in NCT00061087, and review of the full results of NCT00048360
- Detailed mechanism of action data from DrugBank
- A patient-selection and monitoring plan covering seizure risk, blood pressure, and comorbid substance use or eating disorders
- Singapore approved-indication text for the two licences, and a check of whether an ADHD indication would need a regulatory variation

**Other predicted indications:** ADHD, inattentive type (L3) lacks subtype-specific trials and is a research question only. The remaining predictions (for example faciodigitogenital syndrome, chondromyxoid fibroma, hypervitaminosis) have no supporting evidence or plausible mechanism and should be held.
