# Fingolimod: From Relapsing-Remitting Multiple Sclerosis to Borderline Ovarian Serous Tumor

## One-Sentence Summary

Fingolimod is an oral sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) receptor modulator, originally used to treat relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **borderline ovarian serous tumor**, but the evidence is **model prediction only, with no clinical trials and no publications** linked to this indication.
The only supporting material in the compiled data is a small body of preclinical cancer research (2 publications) attached to other, related ovarian predictions.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Based on general knowledge, fingolimod is an S1P receptor modulator. It traps lymphocytes in lymphoid organs, which is how it reduces inflammatory attacks in multiple sclerosis.

S1P signalling has also been linked to the biology of ovarian epithelial tumours, which offers a possible, but unconfirmed, link to this prediction. The compiled data do not verify this link. Multiple sclerosis and ovarian tumours are also very different diseases, and the model score probably reflects proximity in the knowledge graph rather than a proven mechanism.

The other top-ranked predictions are almost all ovarian neoplasms (mostly benign), which suggests the model is picking up one broad "ovarian tumour" signal. Two preclinical studies are relevant to that wider theme:

- **PMID 30120964 (2018):** FTY720 (fingolimod) enhanced the anti-tumour activity of carboplatin and tamoxifen in a patient-derived xenograft model of ovarian cancer.
- **PMID 30388910 (2019):** fingolimod augmented pemetrexed killing of non-small cell lung cancer cells and ovarian cancer cells, and overcame resistance to ERBB inhibitors.

Both studies are preclinical. They suggest a chemosensitising effect in cancer models, not a direct effect on borderline serous tumours.

## Safety Considerations

The package insert warnings and contraindications were not available in the compiled data, so please refer to the package insert for the official safety information.

Supplementary information from other sources:

- **Possible disease-related contraindications (knowledge-graph data, not label text):** liver failure, kidney disease, diabetes mellitus and diabetic ketoacidosis, lung disease, leukopenia, uveitis and other eye inflammation, macular oedema (retinal and cystoid), atrioventricular block, and sick sinus syndrome. These should be confirmed against the official label.
- **Drug interactions:** 100 interactions were identified; the 20 reviewed include these.
  - **Major:** systemic and ophthalmic corticosteroids (hydrocortisone, prednisone, dexamethasone, betamethasone, budesonide, triamcinolone), clarithromycin, levofloxacin, ondansetron, dolasetron, palonosetron, and cisapride.
  - **Moderate:** famotidine, ranitidine, cimetidine, nizatidine, loperamide, and naltrexone.
  - **Minor:** metronidazole.

Fingolimod lowers circulating lymphocytes, which is a concern for any population with immune or haematological vulnerability.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The prediction rests on a model score alone (L5), with no trials and no publications for borderline ovarian serous tumor. The only related data are preclinical chemosensitisation studies, which are indirect. A systemic immunomodulator with cardiac, ocular and infection-related risks is hard to justify for a low-grade or borderline tumour without supporting data.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Mechanism of action data, and an analysis of whether S1P signalling is actually relevant to borderline serous tumours
- Preclinical studies in models of borderline or low-grade serous ovarian tumours
- Package insert warnings and contraindications from the HSA, so the safety screen can be completed
- A risk-benefit assessment for this patient group, covering lymphopenia, cardiac conduction, and macular oedema
- A check of the related predictions (such as serous neoplasm), where the preclinical evidence is stronger, as possible research questions
