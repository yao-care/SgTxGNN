## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Based on the known pharmacology of the drug class, ketorolac is a non-selective COX-1/COX-2 inhibitor. By blocking prostaglandin synthesis, it reduces the peripheral and central sensitization and the neurogenic inflammation involved in migraine and tension-type headache.

Its original indications are inflammatory conditions of the ocular surface, where reducing prostaglandin-driven inflammation and itching is the goal. The same anti-prostaglandin action underlies its use as an analgesic, so extending it to acute headache is a plausible and well-established step. Parenteral ketorolac is already used in emergency departments for acute migraine and tension-type headache, as reflected in the trials and reviews below.

Two other predictions were also generated, but both are weak. *Rosacea conjunctivitis* (score 99.40%) has no trials or literature, and topical NSAIDs carry a corneal toxicity risk on an already compromised ocular surface. *Trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia* (score 99.39%) is mechanistically plausible, but cluster headache generally responds poorly to standard NSAIDs, and the only trial was withdrawn. Both are rated **Hold**.

## Safety Considerations

- **Contraindications / conditions of concern (from knowledge-graph links, not label text)**: hypertension, gastric and peptic ulcer disease, liver disease, gallbladder disease, rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, asthma, diabetes mellitus and diabetic ketoacidosis, blood coagulation disorders and hemophilia, stroke and cerebrovascular disorders, myocardial infarction and other cardiovascular or heart disease, anemia and hemoglobinopathy, impaired renal function, and dry eye syndrome.
- **Drug Interactions**: 222 interactions are recorded. Examples include:
  - **Major**: acetylsalicylic acid
  - **Moderate**: corticosteroids (betamethasone, budesonide, dexamethasone, hydrocortisone), sulfonylureas (acetohexamide, chlorpropamide, glimepiride, glipizide, glyburide), exenatide, kanamycin, levofloxacin, balsalazide, fenfluramine and dexfenfluramine
  - **Minor**: H2 blockers (famotidine, ranitidine, cimetidine), linaclotide
- **Additional considerations**: Class risks include GI bleeding, renal toxicity, cardiovascular risk and bleeding, so a short course (generally 5 days or less) is advised, with caution in renal impairment, in patients on anticoagulants or antiplatelets, and in the elderly. A published case report describes lithium toxicity when ketorolac was co-administered. Repeated NSAID use also raises the risk of medication-overuse headache.

Please also refer to the package insert, since label warnings were not available in the data compiled for this report.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Proceed with Guardrails**

**Rationale:**
Multiple completed Phase 3/4 trials, several systematic reviews and meta-analyses, and guideline-level literature support parenteral ketorolac for acute migraine and tension-type headache, and the COX-inhibition mechanism is plausible. The significant GI, renal, cardiovascular and bleeding risks and the large number of drug interactions require strict controls. Ketorolac is already registered in Singapore in injectable form.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Retrieve the HSA package insert to confirm approved indications, warnings and contraindications. This was a blocking data gap for safety screening.
- Obtain official mechanism of action data from DrugBank.
- Define dose, route and duration limits (short course) and exclusion criteria, including renal impairment, bleeding risk, ulcer history, cardiovascular disease, and anticoagulant or antiplatelet use.
- Review concomitant medications against the interaction list, with particular attention to aspirin, corticosteroids and lithium.
- Check whether a headache indication is already covered by the labels of the registered injectable products, and confirm which routes (for example, intranasal) are actually available locally.
- Keep *rosacea conjunctivitis* and *trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia* on Hold until supporting clinical evidence emerges.
