# Atovaquone: From Malaria and Pneumocystosis to Leprosy

## One-Sentence Summary

Atovaquone is an antiparasitic and anti-Pneumocystis drug, originally used to treat *Plasmodium falciparum* malaria and pneumocystosis.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Currently, detailed mechanism of action data is not available. Based on known information, atovaquone is an antiparasitic agent whose efficacy in malaria and pneumocystosis is established. Its approved Singapore product is MALARONE, an atovaquone-containing product.

The data compiled for this report did not establish a direct mechanistic link between atovaquone and leprosy. Leprosy is a bacterial (mycobacterial) infection, whereas atovaquone's established uses are against protozoa and *Pneumocystis*. The high TxGNN score appears to reflect network proximity, since both are anti-infective, rather than clinical or experimental evidence. The only paper retrieved concerns clofazimine, a leprosy drug, for *Babesia microti* infection. It mentions atovaquone only as a drug that babesiosis can become resistant to, so it does not support atovaquone for leprosy.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The prediction is computational only (evidence level L5). There are no registered trials and no relevant literature, and no mechanistic link between atovaquone and leprosy has been shown. The high score most likely reflects the drug's anti-infective class rather than real efficacy.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Mechanism of action data for atovaquone
- In vitro activity data against *Mycobacterium leprae*, or preclinical studies, to establish biological plausibility
- Package insert warnings and contraindications from the Singapore regulator (HSA) to allow safety screening
- Any clinical or observational data on atovaquone in leprosy; none has been found

**Other predicted indications to consider:** Among the other predictions, **toxoplasmosis** (Phase 2 randomized trial in toxoplasmic encephalitis, Level L2) and **ocular toxoplasmosis** (small clinical series and case reports, Level L3) have the most supporting evidence. Both are already partly established alternative uses and would suit a separate evaluation. The **nocardiosis** literature points to a negative signal: nocardial infections increased in the era of atovaquone prophylaxis. The remaining predictions (facial nerve palsy due to herpes zoster, acne, and the myiasis group) have no supporting evidence.
