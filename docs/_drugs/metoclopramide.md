# Metoclopramide: From Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease to Gastric Ulcer

## One-Sentence Summary

Metoclopramide is a prokinetic and antiemetic drug, originally used for gastroesophageal reflux disease and reflux esophagitis.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **gastric ulcer**,
but the supporting evidence is thin: **1 clinical trial** (a procedural, endoscopy-premedication study) and **20 publications**, mostly older reviews on motility disorders.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Based on known pharmacology, metoclopramide is a dopamine D2 antagonist and 5-HT4 agonist that increases upper gastrointestinal motility, raises lower oesophageal sphincter tone and speeds gastric emptying. This is how it helps in reflux disease.

Gastric ulcer is a neighbouring upper-GI condition. Delayed gastric emptying and duodenogastric (bile) reflux often accompany it, so a motility-based benefit is plausible. However, metoclopramide does not suppress acid or promote mucosal healing. At best it offers symptomatic support alongside standard ulcer therapy, not direct ulcer treatment. The high TxGNN score likely reflects network proximity between reflux and ulcer diseases rather than demonstrated efficacy.

Other predictions follow the same pattern. Gastroduodenitis (99.90%), peptic ulcer disease (99.85%) and gastrojejunal ulcer (99.78%) are supported mainly by indirect motility literature. Peptic ulcer perforation and duodenal obstruction raise safety concerns, since prokinetics are contraindicated in perforation and mechanical obstruction. Achlorhydria and cascade stomach have no meaningful mechanistic link.

## Safety Considerations

- **Contraindications flagged by the knowledge graph** (not taken from label text): hypertension, epilepsy, phaeochromocytoma and paraganglioma, porphyria, active peptic ulcer disease, Parkinson disease and parkinsonian disorders, tardive dyskinesia, torsion dystonia, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, cirrhosis of liver, impaired renal function, anxiety and related disorders, and several others. The listing of **active peptic ulcer disease** is directly relevant to this repurposing question and needs careful review.
- **Mechanical obstruction and perforation:** prokinetics should not be used in these settings, which makes the peptic ulcer perforation and duodenal obstruction predictions unsuitable to pursue.
- **Drug interactions:** 100 interactions were found. The main ones are:
  - **Major:** tramadol, amisulpride.
  - **Moderate:** opioids (fentanyl, alfentanil, codeine, dihydrocodeine), sedating antihistamines (chlorpheniramine, cetirizine), alprazolam, amitriptyline, amobarbital, amantadine, abiraterone, ethanol, and others.

Please refer to the package insert for authoritative warnings and contraindications.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The TxGNN score is very high, but the only registered trial tests endoscopy premedication, not ulcer treatment, and the literature is mostly older reviews of motility disorders. Metoclopramide has no acid-suppressive or mucosal-healing action, and the knowledge graph lists active peptic ulcer disease as a contraindication. The evidence supports at most adjunctive symptom relief.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Package insert warnings and contraindications from HSA (a blocking gap for safety screening)
- Mechanism of action data (for example from DrugBank)
- Clarification of the contraindication on active peptic ulcer disease
- A clinical question restricted to a defined subpopulation (for example ulcer with delayed gastric emptying), supported by controlled trial data
- A clear plan for how metoclopramide would fit alongside standard acid-suppressive therapy
