# Prednisone: From Autoimmune and Inflammatory Diseases to Alopecia Areata

## One-Sentence Summary

Prednisone is a systemic glucocorticoid originally used for autoimmune and inflammatory conditions (such as Crohn disease and adrenocortical insufficiency) and for certain leukaemias.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **alopecia areata**,
with **no registered clinical trials** but **20 publications** currently supporting this direction, including one randomised controlled trial (prednisone combined with methotrexate).

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Currently, detailed mechanism of action data is not available. Based on known information, prednisone is a systemic glucocorticoid, its efficacy in autoimmune and inflammatory disease has been established, and mechanistically it may be applicable to alopecia areata.

Alopecia areata is a T-cell mediated autoimmune attack on hair follicles, with collapse of the follicle's immune privilege. Prednisone suppresses T-cell activation and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which is biologically consistent with this disease mechanism. Many of prednisone's original indications are also autoimmune or inflammatory, so the prediction fits its established pharmacological profile.

The evidence also shows limits. Systemic steroids are already used clinically for alopecia areata, but hair loss often returns after withdrawal, and cumulative steroid toxicity restricts long-term use. A 1977 follow-up of 18 patients treated with alternate-day prednisone found that the initial response did not translate into substantial long-term benefit.

## Safety Considerations

- **Contraindication signals from the knowledge graph (not label text)**: The model's knowledge graph links prednisone to hypertension, congestive heart failure, hyperglycaemia, epilepsy, herpes simplex keratitis, measles, urea cycle disorders (carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I deficiency, hyperargininemia, citrullinemia), and a range of pituitary hormone deficiency and hypogonadotropic hypogonadism conditions. These should be verified against the product label.
- **Safety signals in the alopecia areata literature**: The 1976 follow-up report noted acne, obesity, lenticular opacities and mild hypertension in patients treated with prednisone.
- **Drug Interactions**: 100 interactions were found. Examples include:
  - Major: adalimumab
  - Moderate: zidovudine, isotretinoin, acarbose, acebutolol, acetazolamide, ketorolac, ibuprofen, nifedipine, ethinylestradiol, butalbital, albiglutide, aldesleukin, alemtuzumab, aliskiren, tetanus toxoid vaccine
  - Minor: formoterol, salbutamol

Please refer to the package insert for the full warnings and contraindications, as the label text was not available for this report.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Proceed with Guardrails**

**Rationale:**
The mechanism is biologically coherent and systemic steroids are already used for alopecia areata. However, the only randomised evidence tests prednisone as an add-on to methotrexate, with no registered clinical trials of prednisone itself, and the long-term benefit is limited by relapse and cumulative steroid toxicity. Evidence supports cautious, short-course or combination use rather than broad adoption.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Package insert warnings and contraindications from the HSA (currently missing, and a blocking gap for safety screening)
- Detailed mechanism of action data (MOA) from DrugBank
- The approved indication text for the Singapore licence SIN04379P
- Controlled trial data on prednisone alone, including relapse rates after withdrawal
- A safety monitoring plan for long-term steroid exposure, particularly for patients with hypertension, hyperglycaemia or heart failure
- Review of interactions with concurrent therapies such as JAK inhibitors (e.g. baricitinib) used in severe disease
